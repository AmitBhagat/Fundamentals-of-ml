Chapter 98: Floating Point and Machine Epsilon

Prerequisite

  • Binary Representation: Understanding how integers are stored in base-2 (bits).
  • Scientific Notation: Familiarity with the significand-exponent format, specifically m×βem \times \beta^em×βe.
  • Precision vs. Accuracy: The distinction between how many digits you can track versus how close you are to the "true" value.

Analogy

Dealing with a laptop that has hit its limit is a masterclass in compromise. When you are pushing your hardware to the edge, your computer doesn't have an infinite capacity to track every single click or background process with perfect fidelity. It has a "resolution" of attention.

In the world of Floating Point math, your computer is that struggling laptop. It wants to represent numbers ranging from the size of a subatomic particle to the width of the observable universe, but it only has a fixed amount of "RAM" (bits) to do it. To manage this, it uses a sliding scale. When the numbers get massive, the laptop stops caring about the tiny details because it's too busy keeping the big ones afloat. Machine Epsilon is the "smallest possible update" your laptop can actually register. If you try to make a change smaller than that, the system simply doesn't react—it’s the digital equivalent of clicking a button on a frozen screen; the hardware literally doesn't have the "resources" to notice you moved the needle.

The Math Link

In a normalized floating-point system F⊂R\mathcal{F} \subset \mathbb{R}F⊂R, a number xxx is represented as:

x=±d0.d1d2...dp−1×βex = \pm d_0 . d_1 d_2 ... d_{p-1} \times \beta^ex=±d0​.d1​d2​...dp−1​×βe

Where:

  • β∈Z,β≥2\beta \in \mathbb{Z}, \beta \geq 2β∈Z,β≥2 is the base (radix).
  • p∈Z+p \in \mathbb{Z}^+p∈Z+ is the precision (number of digits in the significand).
  • e∈[emin,emax]e \in [e_{min}, e_{max}]e∈[emin​,emax​] is the exponent.
  • di∈{0,1,...,β−1}d_i \in \{0, 1, ..., \beta-1\}di​∈{0,1,...,β−1} are the digits, with d0≠0d_0 \neq 0d0​=0 for normalized numbers.

Machine Epsilon (ϵmach\epsilon_{mach}ϵmach​) is defined as the distance between 1.01.01.0 and the next larger representable number in the system F\mathcal{F}F. Formally:

ϵmach=min⁡{δ∈R+∣fl(1.0+δ)>1.0}\epsilon_{mach} = \min \{ \delta \in \mathbb{R}^+ \mid \text{fl}(1.0 + \delta) > 1.0 \}ϵmach​=min{δ∈R+∣fl(1.0+δ)>1.0}

Derivation: Consider the representation of 1.01.01.0 in base β\betaβ with precision ppp: 1.0=1.00...0×β01.0 = 1.00...0 \times \beta^01.0=1.00...0×β0 The very next representable number is found by incrementing the smallest digit in the significand (the p−1p-1p−1 position): 1.0next=(1.00...0+0.00...1)×β0=1+β−(p−1)1.0_{next} = (1.00...0 + 0.00...1) \times \beta^0 = 1 + \beta^{-(p-1)}1.0next​=(1.00...0+0.00...1)×β0=1+β−(p−1) Therefore, the gap is: ϵmach=(1+β−(p−1))−1=β1−p\epsilon_{mach} = (1 + \beta^{-(p-1)}) - 1 = \beta^{1-p}ϵmach​=(1+β−(p−1))−1=β1−p

In the Analogy, β1−p\beta^{1-p}β1−p represents the hardware's "minimum detectable click." If your "tabs" (precision ppp) take up too much memory, the gaps between what the hardware can track grow larger.

THE INTUITION Think of Machine Epsilon as the "ruler markings" on your laptop's performance monitor. If your ruler only has markings every 1cm, you can't measure a millimeter. In ML, if your gradients are smaller than ϵmach\epsilon_{mach}ϵmach​, your model effectively stops learning because the weights can't "see" the update.

Let's Run the Numbers

1. The 'Too Many Tabs' Problem

You have so many Chrome tabs open that your laptop's memory is nearly full. You try to open one tiny "About Us" page (1×10−81 \times 10^{-8}1×10−8), but your system's current resolution is only 1×10−71 \times 10^{-7}1×10−7.

The Setup: Assume a toy 32-bit system where β=2\beta = 2β=2 and p=24p = 24p=24. We want to find the gap at 1.01.01.0.

The Calculation: ϵmach=21−24=2−23\epsilon_{mach} = 2^{1-24} = 2^{-23}ϵmach​=21−24=2−23 Using the approximation 210≈1032^{10} \approx 10^3210≈103: 2−23=2−3×(2−10)2≈0.125×(10−3)2=1.25×10−72^{-23} = 2^{-3} \times (2^{-10})^2 \approx 0.125 \times (10^{-3})^2 = 1.25 \times 10^{-7}2−23=2−3×(2−10)2≈0.125×(10−3)2=1.25×10−7

The Story: Because your "tabs" (precision) limit you to a resolution of 1.25×10−71.25 \times 10^{-7}1.25×10−7, adding a tab that only requires 10−810^{-8}10−8 units of memory results in: fl(1.0+10−8)=1.0\text{fl}(1.0 + 10^{-8}) = 1.0fl(1.0+10−8)=1.0 The laptop literally does not change state. The "About Us" page didn't register.

2. The 'Restart' Hope

You've just restarted. The system is clean (small numbers/low exponent). You want to see if the smallest possible background task (2−52^{-5}2−5) can be detected when the system is just idling (202^{0}20).

The Setup: We use a 5-bit "mini-laptop" system: β=2,p=3\beta = 2, p = 3β=2,p=3.

The Calculation: ϵmach=21−3=2−2=0.25\epsilon_{mach} = 2^{1-3} = 2^{-2} = 0.25ϵmach​=21−3=2−2=0.25 Check if an update δ=0.1\delta = 0.1δ=0.1 is registered: fl(1.0+0.1)  ⟹  Is 0.1>0.25? No.\text{fl}(1.0 + 0.1) \implies \text{Is } 0.1 > 0.25? \text{ No.}fl(1.0+0.1)⟹Is 0.1>0.25? No.

The Story: Even with a "Restart" (clean state), if your hardware is weak (low ppp), you still have a massive Epsilon. The 0.10.10.1 update is ignored by the hardware, and the state remains 1.01.01.0. You need better "hardware" (more bits) to see that tiny task.

3. The Hardware Update

You've upgraded your laptop. You now have a 64-bit architecture (p=53p = 53p=53). You want to see if this "Hardware Update" allows you to track a microscopic system tweak.

The Setup: β=2,p=53\beta = 2, p = 53β=2,p=53.

The Calculation: ϵmach=21−53=2−52\epsilon_{mach} = 2^{1-53} = 2^{-52}ϵmach​=21−53=2−52 2−52≈2.22×10−162^{-52} \approx 2.22 \times 10^{-16}2−52≈2.22×10−16

The Story: With the "Hardware Update," your laptop's resolution is now incredibly fine. You can now track updates as small as 0.0000000000000002220.0000000000000002220.000000000000000222. Most ML "background tasks" (gradients) will now be safely captured without the system "freezing" or rounding them to zero.

Machine Epsilon is relative, not absolute. While ϵmach\epsilon_{mach}ϵmach​ defines the gap at 1.01.01.0, the absolute gap between representable numbers scales with the exponent. Specifically, the gap near a number xxx is approximately ∣x∣⋅ϵmach|x| \cdot \epsilon_{mach}∣x∣⋅ϵmach​. In deep networks with large weight values, your "effective" precision drops significantly, leading to catastrophic cancellation.

ML Applications

  1. Mixed Precision Training: Using FP16 (Half-precision) instead of FP32 to speed up training. This increases ϵmach\epsilon_{mach}ϵmach​ from ≈10−7\approx 10^{-7}≈10−7 to ≈10−3\approx 10^{-3}≈10−3, requiring techniques like Loss Scaling to prevent gradients from underflowing to zero.
  2. Optimizer Stability: In algorithms like Adam or RMSProp, a small constant ϵ\epsilonϵ (usually 1e−81e-81e−8) is added to the denominator to prevent division by zero. This ϵ\epsilonϵ must be chosen relative to the precision of the floating-point format being used.
  3. Softmax Temperature: When calculating exp(xi)/∑exp(xj)exp(x_i) / \sum exp(x_j)exp(xi​)/∑exp(xj​), very large xix_ixi​ values can lead to "overflow," while very small differences between xix_ixi​ can be lost if they fall below the relative machine epsilon of the largest value in the vector.
  4. Gradient Clipping: When gradients explode, they reach exponents where the gaps between representable numbers are huge. Clipping ensures the values stay in a range where the floating-point "density" is high enough for meaningful updates.
  5. Initialization Schemes: He or Glorot initialization ensures that weights are scaled such that the variance of activations remains stable. Without this, activations could scale to a range where ϵmach\epsilon_{mach}ϵmach​ causes "vanishing" updates in the significand.

Debugging Tip: If your loss curve is a perfectly horizontal line despite a non-zero learning rate, check if your gradient updates are smaller than the weight values multiplied by ϵmach\epsilon_{mach}ϵmach​. If they are, you're clicking on a frozen laptop—nothing will ever change.
