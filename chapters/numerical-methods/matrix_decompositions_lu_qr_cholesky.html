Chapter 100: Matrix Decompositions (LU, QR, Cholesky)

Prerequisite

  • Matrix Multiplication: Understanding how C=ABC = ABC=AB is formed by the dot product of rows and columns.
  • System of Linear Equations: Familiarity with representing Ax=bAx = bAx=b and the concept of "solving for xxx."
  • Dot Products and Orthogonality: Knowing when two vectors are perpendicular (u⋅v=0u \cdot v = 0u⋅v=0).

Analogy

Think of your raw data matrix as a massive, overflowing wardrobe. Right now, it is a chaotic pile of fabric—shirts mixed with winter coats, belts tangled with formal trousers. It is technically "all there," but if you need to find a specific outfit for a specific weather condition, you have to dig through the entire mess. This is computationally expensive and mentally draining.

Matrix Decomposition is the professional act of organizing that wardrobe. We aren't changing the volume of clothes you own; we are simply breaking the "pile" down into structured, specialized components. By decomposing a matrix, we transform a single complex block of information into a series of simpler, ordered sub-sections. Once the wardrobe is categorized, solving complex problems (like finding the right attire) becomes a matter of looking at the specific shelf where that category lives, rather than searching the whole room.

The Math Link

In formal terms, decomposition factorizes a matrix AAA into a product of simpler matrices. The three primary methods represent different ways of sorting the "fabric" of your data.

1. LU Decomposition

We factorize a square matrix A∈Rn×nA \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}A∈Rn×n into a Lower triangular matrix LLL and an Upper triangular matrix UUU: A=LUA = LUA=LU Where Lij=0L_{ij} = 0Lij​=0 for i<ji < ji<j and Uij=0U_{ij} = 0Uij​=0 for i>ji > ji>j. This is derived via Gaussian Elimination where we track the multipliers used to zero out elements below the pivot.

2. QR Decomposition

We factorize A∈Rm×nA \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}A∈Rm×n into an orthogonal matrix QQQ and an upper triangular matrix RRR: A=QRA = QRA=QR Where QTQ=IQ^T Q = IQTQ=I (the columns are orthonormal) and RRR is upper triangular. This is derived using the Gram-Schmidt process: uk=ak−∑j=1k−1projuj(ak),ek=uk∥uk∥\mathbf{u}_k = \mathbf{a}_k - \sum_{j=1}^{k-1} \text{proj}_{\mathbf{u}_j} (\mathbf{a}_k), \quad \mathbf{e}_k = \frac{\mathbf{u}_k}{\|\mathbf{u}_k\|}uk​=ak​−∑j=1k−1​projuj​​(ak​),ek​=∥uk​∥uk​​

3. Cholesky Decomposition

For a Hermitian, positive-definite matrix AAA, we decompose it into a lower triangular matrix LLL and its conjugate transpose: A=LLTA = LL^TA=LLT The components are derived such that Lii=Aii−∑k=1i−1Lik2L_{ii} = \sqrt{A_{ii} - \sum_{k=1}^{i-1} L_{ik}^2}Lii​=Aii​−∑k=1i−1​Lik2​​.

THE INTUITION If you have a massive system to solve, don't attack the whole matrix. LU is for general efficiency, QR is for stability when things are messy, and Cholesky is the "shortcut" for when your data is perfectly symmetrical and well-behaved.

Let's Run the Numbers

Example 1: The 'Ironed' Section (LU Decomposition)

You have a pile of work shirts (AAA) that need to be categorized into "The Effort" (LLL, the process of folding) and "The Result" (UUU, the crisp shirts on the shelf). Given A=(23821)A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 8 & 21 \end{pmatrix}A=(28​321​), we find LLL and UUU.

  1. Set U1j=A1j  ⟹  U=(230u22)U_{1j} = A_{1j} \implies U = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 0 & u_{22} \end{pmatrix}U1j​=A1j​⟹U=(20​3u22​​).
  2. Find the multiplier for the second row: l21=A21U11=82=4l_{21} = \frac{A_{21}}{U_{11}} = \frac{8}{2} = 4l21​=U11​A21​​=28​=4.
  3. Update u22=A22−l21U12=21−(4)(3)=9u_{22} = A_{22} - l_{21}U_{12} = 21 - (4)(3) = 9u22​=A22​−l21​U12​=21−(4)(3)=9. Result: L=(1041),U=(2309)L = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 4 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, U = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 9 \end{pmatrix}L=(14​01​),U=(20​39​) The Story: By separating the "ironing steps" (LLL) from the "final state" (UUU), we can now solve any daily outfit requirement by simply looking at the top shelf (UUU) and tracing back the steps.

You want to separate what stays from what goes, ensuring every kept item is "orthogonal"—meaning no two pieces of clothing serve the exact same redundant purpose. Given A=(1101)A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}A=(10​11​).

  2. u2=a2−(a2⋅q1)q1=(11)−(1)(10)=(01)\mathbf{u}_2 = \mathbf{a}_2 - (\mathbf{a}_2 \cdot \mathbf{q}_1)\mathbf{q}_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} - (1)\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}u2​=a2​−(a2​⋅q1​)q1​=(11​)−(1)(10​)=(01​). Example 3: The 'Seasonal' Shift (Cholesky Decomposition)

Your wardrobe is perfectly mirrored—winter gear exactly balances summer gear (Symmetric Positive Definite). We only need to store one "half" of the logic to know the whole state. Given A=(4121237)A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 12 \\ 12 & 37 \end{pmatrix}A=(412​1237​).

  1. L11=A11=4=2L_{11} = \sqrt{A_{11}} = \sqrt{4} = 2L11​=A11​​=4​=2.
  2. L21=A21L11=122=6L_{21} = \frac{A_{21}}{L_{11}} = \frac{12}{2} = 6L21​=L11​A21​​=212​=6.
  3. L22=A22−L212=37−36=1L_{22} = \sqrt{A_{22} - L_{21}^2} = \sqrt{37 - 36} = 1L22​=A22​−L212​​=37−36​=1. Result: L=(2061)L = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 6 & 1 \end{pmatrix}L=(26​01​) The Story: Because the wardrobe was symmetric, we only saved half the space (LLL) and still know that LTL^TLT completes the picture perfectly.

CRITICAL INSIGHT: LU decomposition can fail or become numerically unstable if a pivot element is zero or very small. In professional ML libraries, we almost always use Pivoting (PA=LUPA = LUPA=LU), which physically swaps rows to keep the largest values on the diagonal, ensuring the "wardrobe" doesn't collapse under its own weight.

ML Applications

  1. Linear Regression via QR: In OLS, solving (XTX)β=XTy(X^T X)\beta = X^T y(XTX)β=XTy can be unstable. Decomposing X=QRX = QRX=QR allows us to solve Rβ=QTyR\beta = Q^T yRβ=QTy, which is numerically superior and avoids the inversion of the Gramian matrix.
  2. Gaussian Processes: Cholesky decomposition is used to sample from multivariate normal distributions and to compute the log-determinant of the covariance matrix Σ\SigmaΣ.
  3. Optimization (Newton's Method): In training deep networks or logistic regression, the Hessian matrix is often decomposed using LU or Cholesky to find the update direction for weights.
  4. Latent Semantic Analysis (LSA): While SVD is common, QR is often used as a preprocessing step to reduce dimensionality and orthogonalize feature vectors in NLP.
  5. State-Space Models: In Time-Series analysis (like Kalman Filters), matrix decompositions are performed at every time step to update the covariance of the hidden state.

Debugging Tip: If your Cholesky decomposition throws a "Matrix is not positive definite" error, check your data for redundant/highly correlated features or negative eigenvalues; your "wardrobe" essentially has conflicting items that make it impossible to sort symmetrically.
