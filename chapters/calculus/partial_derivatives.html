Chapter 38: Partial Derivatives

Prerequisite

  • Single-Variable Calculus: Understanding the derivative as the instantaneous rate of change for functions of the form f(x)f(x)f(x).
  • Multivariable Functions: Familiarity with functions that accept multiple independent inputs, typically denoted as f(x,y,z,… )f(x, y, z, \dots)f(x,y,z,…).
  • Limits and Continuity: A basic grasp of how a function behaves as its inputs approach a specific point in space.

Analogy

Managing a complex machine learning model is often like managing a modern Bank Passbook. In a perfect world, your balance is just one number, but in reality, your financial standing is a result of multiple independent streams: interest credits, ATM withdrawals, monthly fees, and direct deposits.

When you stand at that kiosk to update your passbook, you aren't just looking at the final balance; you are looking for the "why" behind the change. If your balance dropped by 500500500, you need to isolate which specific "input"—the rent check or the weekend splurge—caused that shift. A partial derivative is the mathematical equivalent of looking at your passbook and asking: "If I keep every other transaction exactly the same, but I increase my monthly interest credit by just one dollar, how much will my final balance change?" It is the art of freezing the world to see how one specific lever moves the needle.

The Math Link

In formal terms, let fff be a scalar-valued function of nnn variables, defined on an open set S⊆Rn\mathcal{S} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^nS⊆Rn. The partial derivative of fff with respect to the iii-th variable xix_ixi​ at the point a=(a1,a2,…,an)\mathbf{a} = (a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n)a=(a1​,a2​,…,an​) is defined as the limit:

∂f∂xi(a)=lim⁡h→0f(a1,…,ai+h,…,an)−f(a1,…,ai,…,an)h\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(\mathbf{a}) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a_1, \dots, a_i + h, \dots, a_n) - f(a_1, \dots, a_i, \dots, a_n)}{h}∂xi​∂f​(a)=limh→0​hf(a1​,…,ai​+h,…,an​)−f(a1​,…,ai​,…,an​)​

To derive this logic for a multivariable system where z=f(x,y)z = f(x, y)z=f(x,y), we treat the variable not being differentiated as a constant. If we are differentiating with respect to xxx, we treat yyy as a fixed value y0y_0y0​. The derivation follows the standard difference quotient:

  1. Isolate the Variable: Define a single-variable function g(x)=f(x,y0)g(x) = f(x, y_0)g(x)=f(x,y0​).
  2. Apply Power/Chain Rules: Compute g′(x)g'(x)g′(x) using standard rules ∀x∈R\forall x \in \mathbb{R}∀x∈R where the derivative exists.
  3. Result: The resulting expression ∂f∂x\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}∂x∂f​ represents the slope of the tangent line to the surface z=f(x,y)z = f(x, y)z=f(x,y) in the direction of the xxx-axis.

In our analogy:

  • f(x1,x2,…,xn)f(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n)f(x1​,x2​,…,xn​) is the Final Passbook Balance.
  • xix_ixi​ is a Specific Transaction Type (e.g., Interest Rate).
  • ∂f∂xi\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}∂xi​∂f​ is the Sensitivity of your balance to that specific transaction.

THE INTUITION Think of partial derivatives as "blinders." You are intentionally ignoring the chaos of the other variables to see the pure, unadulterated influence of a single factor. If you change xxx and yyy at the same time, you'll never know who to blame for the error.

Example 1: Standing in the machine queue

You are standing at the bank kiosk. Your total satisfaction SSS depends on the number of people in the queue (qqq) and the cooling temperature of the lobby (ttt). The satisfaction function is S(q,t)=100−q2−0.5t2S(q, t) = 100 - q^2 - 0.5t^2S(q,t)=100−q2−0.5t2. CRITICAL INSIGHT In ML, we rarely deal with two variables. We deal with millions. The collection of all partial derivatives of a function is the Gradient Vector ∇f\nabla f∇f. A common "gotcha" is forgetting that a partial derivative only gives you the slope in an axis-aligned direction. If you want to move in a diagonal direction, the partial derivative alone isn't enough—you need the full gradient.

ML Applications

  • Gradient Descent in Backpropagation: Partial derivatives of the loss function LLL with respect to each weight wijw_{ij}wij​ and bias bib_ibi​ are calculated to update parameters using wnew=wold−η∂L∂ww_{new} = w_{old} - \eta \frac{\partial L}{\partial w}wnew​=wold​−η∂w∂L​.
  • Jacobian Matrices in Robotics: In inverse kinematics, the Jacobian matrix (a matrix of first-order partial derivatives) maps joint velocities to end-effector velocities in 3D space.
  • Feature Importance in Sensitivity Analysis: By calculating ∂y^∂xi\frac{\partial \hat{y}}{\partial x_i}∂xi​∂y^​​, researchers determine which input feature xix_ixi​ has the greatest marginal impact on the model's prediction y^\hat{y}y^​.
  • Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs): During the backward pass of a convolution layer, partial derivatives are computed with respect to the filter kernels to learn spatial patterns like edges and textures.
  • Regularization Tuning: Partial derivatives of the penalty terms (like L1L1L1 or L2L2L2 norms) are added to the loss gradient to ensure the model weights remain small and avoid overfitting.

Debugging Tip: If your model isn't learning, check your partial derivatives. A "Vanishing Gradient" happens when the partial derivative w.r.t. a weight becomes so small that the update wnew=wold−η⋅0w_{new} = w_{old} - \eta \cdot 0wnew​=wold​−η⋅0 effectively stops the learning process. Always monitor the magnitude of your partials during training.
