Chapter 32: Critical Points

Prerequisite

  • Partial Derivatives: Comfort with calculating ∂f∂xi\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}∂xi​∂f​ for multivariable functions.
  • The Gradient Vector: Understanding that ∇f(x)\nabla f(\mathbf{x})∇f(x) represents the direction of steepest ascent.
  • Scalar Fields: Recognizing how a single output value (error or height) mapped across a domain creates a surface.

Analogy

Navigating a bike through a city on a rainy day isn't about the smooth pavement; it’s about managing the irregularities. As you're riding, your primary focus is the geometry of the road surface. You are constantly scanning for those specific moments where the "slope" of the road changes—the spots where the pavement stops going up and starts going down, or levels out entirely.

In ML, we treat our error functions like that rain-slicked road. A Critical Point is any location on that path where the ground is momentarily level. It is the split second where your tires aren't leaning forward into a descent or straining against an incline. These points are the only places where the "danger" (or the reward) reaches a peak, a trough, or a deceptive plateau. You can't navigate a puddle or a hill without identifying these transitions first, because they dictate whether you're about to accelerate uncontrollably or come to a complete standstill in a deep pool of water.

The Math Link

In a formal sense, we define a critical point by examining the behavior of the gradient of a differentiable function. For a function f:D→Rf: \mathcal{D} \to \mathbb{R}f:D→R where D⊆Rn\mathcal{D} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^nD⊆Rn, a point x∗\mathbf{x}^*x∗ is a critical point if the gradient vector vanishes.

Let f(x)f(\mathbf{x})f(x) be a scalar-valued function of nnn variables x=[x1,x2,…,xn]⊤\mathbf{x} = [x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n]^\topx=[x1​,x2​,…,xn​]⊤. The gradient ∇f\nabla f∇f is defined as the vector of partial derivatives:

∇f(x)=[∂f∂x1,∂f∂x2,…,∂f∂xn]⊤\nabla f(\mathbf{x}) = \left[ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}, \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_2}, \dots, \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_n} \right]^\top∇f(x)=[∂x1​∂f​,∂x2​∂f​,…,∂xn​∂f​]⊤

A point x∗\mathbf{x}^*x∗ is a critical point if:

∇f(x∗)=0\nabla f(\mathbf{x}^*) = \mathbf{0}∇f(x∗)=0

This implies the following system of equations must hold true:

∀i∈{1,…,n},∂f∂xi∣x=x∗=0\forall i \in \{1, \dots, n\}, \quad \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}\bigg|_{\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}^*} = 0∀i∈{1,…,n},∂xi​∂f​​x=x∗​=0

Linking the Symbols to the Road:

  • f(x)f(\mathbf{x})f(x): The elevation of the road surface at any coordinate (x,y)(x, y)(x,y).
  • ∇f(x)\nabla f(\mathbf{x})∇f(x): The "slant" of the pavement under your tires. If it's non-zero, gravity is pulling you in a specific direction.
  • 0\mathbf{0}0: The state of being perfectly level. Your bike would theoretically stay still here without brakes.

THE INTUITION Finding a critical point is like feeling for the moment of weightlessness at the top of a jump or the moment of total suspension at the bottom of a dip. You aren't looking for "flat ground" in general; you are looking for the exact coordinates where the forces of "up" and "down" cancel each other out perfectly.

Let's Run the Numbers

Example 1: The Slalom of Riding a Bike

You are weaving between obstacles on a path defined by f(x,y)=x2+y2f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2f(x,y)=x2+y2. You need to find the point where the ground is flat so you don't slide sideways into a curb.

The Calculation:

  1. Compute the partial derivatives: ∂f∂x=2x,∂f∂y=2y\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 2x, \quad \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = 2y∂x∂f​=2x,∂y∂f​=2y
  2. Set the gradient to zero: ∇f(x,y)=[2x2y]=[00]\nabla f(x, y) = \begin{bmatrix} 2x \\ 2y \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}∇f(x,y)=[2x2y​]=[00​]
  3. Solve the system: 2x=0  ⟹  x=02x = 0 \implies x = 02x=0⟹x=0 2y=0  ⟹  y=02y = 0 \implies y = 02y=0⟹y=0

The Story: The math tells you that the only place your bike won't naturally pull to one side is at the exact origin (0,0)(0, 0)(0,0). Anywhere else, and the "slalom" effect of the curve will force you to steer actively to stay upright.

Example 2: Judging the Depth of a Puddle

You encounter a long, trough-shaped puddle defined by f(x,y)=x2−y2f(x, y) = x^2 - y^2f(x,y)=x2−y2. You need to find the "center" to see if it's the deepest point or just a transition.

The Calculation:

  1. Compute partials: ∂f∂x=2x,∂f∂y=−2y\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 2x, \quad \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = -2y∂x∂f​=2x,∂y∂f​=−2y
  2. Set the gradient to zero: [2x−2y]=[00]\begin{bmatrix} 2x \\ -2y \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}[2x−2y​]=[00​]
  3. Result: x∗=(0,0)\mathbf{x}^* = (0, 0)x∗=(0,0)

The Story: At (0,0)(0, 0)(0,0), the ground is flat, but this is a "saddle point." If you move along the xxx-axis, the water gets deeper. If you move along the yyy-axis, you're actually on a ridge. The math warns you that "flat" doesn't always mean "safe bottom"; it could be the middle of a complex ripple.

Example 3: Navigating the Rainy Day Incline

The road elevation is modeled by f(x,y)=e−(x2+y2)f(x, y) = e^{-(x^2+y^2)}f(x,y)=e−(x2+y2), representing a dry hump in the middle of a flooded street.

The Calculation:

  1. Using the chain rule for partials: ∂f∂x=−2xe−(x2+y2),∂f∂y=−2ye−(x2+y2)\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = -2x e^{-(x^2+y^2)}, \quad \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = -2y e^{-(x^2+y^2)}∂x∂f​=−2xe−(x2+y2),∂y∂f​=−2ye−(x2+y2)
  2. Set to zero: −2xe−(x2+y2)=0and−2ye−(x2+y2)=0-2x e^{-(x^2+y^2)} = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad -2y e^{-(x^2+y^2)} = 0−2xe−(x2+y2)=0and−2ye−(x2+y2)=0
  3. Since eue^ueu is never zero, we must have: −2x=0  ⟹  x=0,−2y=0  ⟹  y=0-2x = 0 \implies x = 0, \quad -2y = 0 \implies y = 0−2x=0⟹x=0,−2y=0⟹y=0

The Story: The critical point is at (0,0)(0, 0)(0,0). Because of the negative exponent, this is the highest point of the hump. This is where you want to put your tires to stay out of the water.

Finding a critical point where ∇f=0\nabla f = \mathbf{0}∇f=0 is only half the battle. In high-dimensional ML landscapes, the vast majority of critical points are saddle points, not local minima. Relying solely on the first derivative (gradient) without considering the second derivative (Hessian matrix) can lead an optimizer to get stuck in a region that is flat but provides zero progress toward minimizing loss.

ML Applications

  1. Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD): The fundamental goal of SGD is to iteratively update weights w\mathbf{w}w until ∇L(w)≈0\nabla L(\mathbf{w}) \approx \mathbf{0}∇L(w)≈0, effectively searching for a critical point in the weight space that corresponds to a low loss value.
  2. Ordinary Least Squares (OLS): In linear regression, we find the closed-form solution by setting the derivative of the Sum of Squared Errors (SSE) with respect to the coefficients β\boldsymbol{\beta}β to zero: ∂∂β∥y−Xβ∥2=0\frac{\partial}{\partial \boldsymbol{\beta}} \| \mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} \|^2 = \mathbf{0}∂β∂​∥y−Xβ∥2=0.
  3. Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs): The training process seeks a Nash Equilibrium, which is a specific type of critical point (a saddle point) in a minimax game between the Generator and the Discriminator.
  4. Variational Autoencoders (VAEs): When maximizing the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO), we are searching for the critical points of the variational objective to find the optimal parameters for the latent distribution.
  5. Principal Component Analysis (PCA): Finding the directions of maximum variance involves identifying the critical points of the variance function under the constraint that the weight vectors have unit norm, typically solved using Lagrange multipliers.

Debugging Tip: If your loss curve goes perfectly flat but your model performance is still terrible, you’ve likely hit a critical point that is a high-level saddle point or a local maximum. Check your gradients; if they are near zero but the loss is high, try injecting noise into your gradients or changing your learning rate to "bump" the optimizer out of that plateau.
