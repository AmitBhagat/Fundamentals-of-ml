<h3>Prerequisite</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Probability (<span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><semantics><mrow><mi>p</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">p</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.625em;vertical-align:-0.1944em;"></span><span class="mord mathnormal">p</span></span></span></span>):</strong> Understanding the likelihood of a class occurring in a set.</li>
<li><strong>Entropy:</strong> The measurement of "Disorder" or "Uncertainty" in a group.</li>
<li><strong>Recursion:</strong> The idea of solving a large problem by breaking it into smaller, identical sub-problems.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Analogy</h2>
<p>Imagine you are playing a game of <strong>"Twenty Questions."</strong> You are trying to guess a mystery object.</p>
<p>If your first question is "Is it made of atoms?", it’s a terrible question because the answer is always "Yes." You haven't gained any information. But if you ask "Is it alive?", you have instantly cut the possibilities in half.</p>
<p>A <strong>Decision Tree</strong> is a machine that plays "Twenty Questions" with your data. It looks at every feature (Age, Salary, Location) and asks: "Which question will split this messy pile of data into two cleaner, more 'Pure' piles?"</p>
<ul>
<li>The <strong>Geometry</strong> of a Decision Tree is a series of <strong>Axis-aligned Cuts</strong>. It’s like taking a block of wood and making straight saw-cuts until you’ve isolated every knot.</li>
</ul>
<hr>

<p><strong>THE INTUITION</strong>
A Decision Tree is <strong>Greedy</strong>. At every step, it only cares about making the <em>next</em> split the best one possible. It doesn't plan for the future. This is why trees can easily "Overfit"—they chase tiny, insignificant patterns in the data until they’ve isolated every single data point into its own leaf.</p>

<h3>Example 3: The Leaf Prediction</h3>
<p>A tree reaches a "Leaf" (a terminal node) where there are 100 samples: 90 "Fraud" and 10 "Safe."</p>
<p><strong>Calculation:</strong>
The tree calculates the majority class.</p>
<ul>
<li>Probability(Fraud) = 0.9.</li>
<li>Prediction = "Fraud".</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>The Story:</strong> The tree doesn't just give a label; it gives a <strong>Confidence</strong>. In this case, it is 90% sure about its decision.</p>

<p><strong>CRITICAL TECHNICAL INSIGHT: High Cardinality Bias</strong>
Decision Trees have a "Crush" on features with many unique values (like "User ID" or "Exact Timestamp"). Because these features can create "Perfect Splits" (one ID per leaf), they look like they have massive Information Gain. In reality, they are just memorizing the data. <strong>Always drop unique identifiers</strong> before training a tree!</p>
